# What Atlas could become

My working interpretation of the original idea is a map of what would make useful medical capabilities possible: what we know, which explanations remain plausible, what is missing, and what we could learn next. The aspiration is to make biological progress easier to reason about across fields. This is a hypothesis about the intended product, to test through use.

The first useful unit is a research question with enough context to compare approaches. A global graph becomes valuable when its individual connections can survive inspection.

## The recurring workflow

1. Choose a capability or question, and specify the biological setting and outcome.
2. Compare possible approaches, their assumptions, and their limits.
3. Trace the relevant claims to particular passages in primary sources.
4. Record what an observation or experiment would change about the interpretation.
5. Add a scoped dependency only when a named review supports that relationship.
6. Revisit the question when new evidence arrives, retaining earlier judgments and disagreements.

The graph should support several routes through incomplete knowledge. Different approaches can have different prerequisites, and a limitation of one approach need not be a limitation of the whole question.

## Categories as views

Subject, biological scale, method, organism or system, and medical application answer different questions. They should be independent annotations. One cell-fate question can appear under both molecular and cellular scales without being duplicated.

Identity is a separate judgment: two descriptions may be equivalent, one may be narrower, or they may share a method. Category overlap alone cannot settle that judgment. Nor can it establish a dependency, an explanation, or research priority.

## Stages and the evidence needed to proceed

| Stage | Deliverable | What would justify the next stage |
|---|---|---|
| First usable slice | A local cell-fate and gene-regulation workbench with scoped claims, source links, multiple facets, proposals, and review history. | A user can follow a question through alternatives to specific evidence and a testable next question. |
| Researcher evaluation | Work through a real disagreement with someone familiar with the field; record corrections and missing context. | The representation helps explain a decision or exposes a missing assumption. Passing software tests alone cannot establish this. |
| Adjacent-domain expansion | Add one neighboring domain, retaining its own systems, methods, and evidence boundaries. Introduce stable external subject identifiers where they solve a demonstrated ambiguity. | Cross-domain links remain meaningful after both sides are reviewed, and the categories still work without duplicate identities. |
| Assisted literature updates | An inbox of candidate sources, claims, and amendments with explicit provenance and review state. | Evaluated extraction and linking errors are understood, review effort is manageable, and new papers can change or withdraw earlier interpretations. |
| Research planning | Compare particular proposed studies by expected information, feasibility, resources, and which interpretations their outcomes would change. | Inputs have identifiable sources and assumptions; a researcher can challenge the comparison and obtain a different result. |
| Broader medical coverage | Multiple maintained collections joined by reviewed connections and common identifiers. | Coverage, disagreement, maintenance burden, and stale evidence are visible. Collection size is not mistaken for completeness. |

Resource planning should describe a particular study in a particular setting. A subject label cannot establish a budget or equipment requirement. A reachability result can help inspect a hypothesis about dependencies; its node count is not a funding recommendation.

## What to learn from the pilot

The pilot asks whether measured cellular state can predict fate, and how to identify regulatory influences on fate transitions. Those questions make it possible to examine a recurring distinction: prediction, inferred association, and evidence about the effect of an intervention can support different conclusions.

Use the [evaluation sheet](evaluation.md) to record a concrete session. Keep the user's decision, sources checked, disputed interpretations, and any proposed experiment. Compare that session with how the same question would have been handled using the papers alone. Record failures, including confusion caused by the workbench.

The initial collection is a deliberately selected demonstration, with publication coverage described in [pilot sources](pilot-sources.md). It has no adjudicated prerequisite graph. Its first test is whether it helps a person formulate a sharper research question and inspect the evidence behind it.
